## Background

The Trident Series 65 recording console was released in 1984, and is probably
best described as an entry level professional console. A fully modular, split
design, it came in numerous configurations (8 bus, 24 bus, etc.) and sometimes
with an integrated patch bay.

I had my first encounter with a Trident 65 (and also my first encounter with 
professional recording studio equipment) as a kid, when I visited a studio in
downtown Cleveland called "The Right Track" (not to be confused with the
studio of the same name in NYC.) The former owner, Bart Koster, told me
recently that the studio actually had two of the consoles (one for each room).

My brief visit to that studio in the 1987 instilled in me a lifetime
fascination and interest in sound production and related technologies (MIDI,
etc.) The first time I saw a computer control a synthesizer was in that
studio, and while that type of equipment was sort of out of reach for most
hobbyists then (not to mention a 10 year old kid!) it made quite an impression
on me at the time.

Fast forward to 2026: I was visiting a studio in NYC to buy a small piece of
gear I saw on Craigslist. Tucked in the corner under a cover was a Trident 24!
The console in question, and the one that is a topic of this Github, was a
28-input, 24-bus model, was in excellent physical condition, and the power
supply and service manual were both present. But was it for sale?

The answer was yes! And while I mulled over whether I needed another console,
(I refurbished a
[Soundcraft Spirit Studio](https://github.com/dialtr/soundcraft-spirit-studio)
and also own a 32-channel Ghost that needs repair) I ultimately determined that
I might never have a chance to have a console like this in such great
condition. We quickly agreed to a fair price, and I took it home!

## Plans

For the Spirit Studio, I aimed at restoring it to its original (or better)
condition, and made few mods. I did replace the faders with models that were
much better than stock, but other than that, I focused on extensive cleaning
and addressing faults.

For the Trident, I am planning to do a little more: in addition to returning
it to its formal glory, I am planning to make some hopefully substantial
upgrades along the way, including selecting some modern op-amps to replace
some or all of the stock TL071 ICs, capacitor upgrades and value changes,
and modifications to the meters to use LED illumination.

Unlike many professional-grade consoles that often use hard-to-find parts
that can also be expensive, the Trident is a desirable platform for upgrades
given that it uses very straightforward components. All of the integrated
circuits used are available today and are not that expensive. Even better,
some modern replacements exist that offer virtually no-compromise improvements
while remaining inexpensive, and also won't require substantial upgrades to
the power supply.

In short, this project is going to be different: I am not only going to fix
it, I am going to (hopefully) make it better and potentially more reliable as
well.

That being said, a huge pet peeve of mine is that many alleged "upgrades" of
audio circuitry are snake-oil. There exist companies that will take your old
gear from you and upgrade them (typically, with modern op-amps, better
capacitors, and other voodoo) and while they shall rename nameless, I have
yet to find a company that will publish specifications related to these
alleged improvements!

Accordingly, I will be conducting experiments and measurements along the way.
Will swapping out chips, capacitors, and other mods live up to the hype?
We're going to find out!
